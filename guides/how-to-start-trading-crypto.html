How to Start Trading Crypto: A Beginner's Step-by-Step Guide

9 MIN READ UPDATED AUGUST 2026

Every major exchange walks you through the same basic on-ramp — sign up, verify, secure, fund, trade. This guide pulls that process into one place, in plain language, so you know what to expect before you hand over your ID or your money.

TIME TO SET UP
15–30 minutes

Step 1: Pick a reliable exchange

Start with a centralized exchange rather than anything decentralized — it's the more forgiving on-ramp while you're still learning. Look for a long track record, clear security practices, and support that actually responds. Compare a couple of exchanges on fees and coin selection before committing; our exchange comparison guides go deeper on that.

Step 2: Create and verify your account

Sign-up itself takes a minute — email, password, agree to terms. The part that takes longer is identity verification (KYC): a government ID and sometimes a selfie. Every reputable exchange requires this now, and skipping it usually caps how much you can deposit or withdraw. Expect anywhere from a few minutes to a day for approval, depending on the exchange and how busy their verification queue is.

Step 3: Secure your account before you fund it

Do this before you add any money, not after:

  • Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA) using an authenticator app rather than SMS where possible
  • Set an anti-phishing code if your exchange offers one, so you can tell real emails from fake ones
  • Use a password you don't reuse anywhere else

Crypto transactions can't be reversed the way a bank transfer can, which is exactly why this step goes before Step 4, not after.

Step 4: Fund your account

You can typically fund an account with fiat (bank transfer, UPI, or card, depending on region) or by transferring crypto in from another wallet or exchange. If you're moving crypto in, pay attention to the network you select — a coin like USDT exists on several different blockchains, and sending on the wrong network can mean the funds don't arrive at all. When in doubt, send a small test amount first.

Step 5: Make your first trade

Most beginners start with spot trading — buying or selling at the current market price and simply holding. You'll usually choose between two order types:

  • Market order — buys or sells immediately at whatever the current price is. Simple, but you don't control the exact price.
  • Limit order — you set the price you're willing to buy or sell at, and the trade only executes if the market reaches it. More control, no guarantee it fills.

Leave margin, futures, and leverage alone until spot trading feels boring. They amplify both gains and losses, and they're not where a first trade should happen.

Step 6: Start small and manage risk

Trade with an amount you could lose without it affecting your life — this isn't pessimism, it's how you buy yourself room to learn without panic. Avoid chasing a coin because it's spiking (FOMO is one of the most common ways beginners lose money), and avoid trading so often that fees quietly eat your account. A small, boring first month is a good sign, not a disappointing one.

Common beginner mistakes

Do this

  • Enable 2FA before you fund the account
  • Start with spot trading, small amounts
  • Double-check the network before any crypto transfer

Avoid this

  • Using high leverage before you understand liquidation
  • Buying purely because a price is spiking
  • Skipping security settings to "set it up later"

Frequently asked questions

How much money do I need to start trading crypto?

Most major exchanges let you start with very small amounts — often the equivalent of a few hundred rupees. There's no meaningful minimum; the point of starting small is to learn the mechanics before you commit more.

Is spot trading or futures trading better for beginners?

Spot trading is the better starting point. It caps your risk at what you put in, while futures and margin trading use leverage that can amplify losses beyond your initial deposit.

Do I really need to complete KYC verification?

On any reputable exchange, yes. Unverified accounts are usually restricted to very low deposit and withdrawal limits, and KYC is what lets the exchange offer fraud protection and support if something goes wrong.
